# Gameplay physics research

Research completed before implementation. The values below are gameplay approximations, not claims that a Minecraft block is a laboratory basketball court. They are chosen to preserve the direction and relative feel of the measured effects while keeping the simulation stable and replayable.

## 1. Shot timing and make probability

NBA 2K does not publish one timeless penalty for a release labelled "slightly early". The timing window, shot animation, rating, contest and game version all matter, so the label is not a portable probability.

NBA2KLab's 2K27 shot-meter test used an automated, fixed-millisecond release rig: 1,600 open shots, 100 attempts per timing cell, one build, one jumper and one floor spot. Relative to the measured center of that jumper's window, the meter-on test made 89% at the two center cells, 55% at 7.5 ms early and 54% at 12.5 ms early. In that tested setup, those early offsets cost about 34–35 percentage points against the center. The same page shows the asymmetry of the tested meter-off boost: early cells were essentially unchanged, while later cells improved. This is useful evidence for a smooth timing penalty, not a universal conversion from the words "slightly early" to a fixed make rate. [NBA2KLab, "NBA 2K27 Shot Meter Boost Test"](https://www.nba2klab.com/shot-meter-test)

Their 2K20 comparison also reports that the penalty depends on skill and distance from the release window: at an 85 three-point rating, the tested slightly-early point made 12% more often than a very-early point, while the tested green-window edge made 18% more often than the slightly-early point. [NBA2KLab, "How Jump Shooting Works in 2K20"](https://www.nba2klab.com/videos/2k20-shooting-comparison)

**Design takeaway:** release quality belongs in one bounded probability function, but the 2K data does not justify importing one game's absolute percentages. `ShotModel` now composes release quality, defender contest, normalized fatigue, distance, airborne state and angular aim error in one pure, testable function. Its result is always clamped to 0.02–0.99 and also drives the seeded physical-dispersion description.

**Implemented calibration (gameplay tuning, not a claim of universal basketball percentages):** with `r = max(0, distanceBlocks - 2)`, the base is `0.96 - 0.018r - 0.003r²`; contest costs `0.36 × contest^1.15`, fatigue costs `0.18 × fatigue^1.35`, airborne state costs 0.025, and aim error costs `min(0.45, 0.0015 × degrees²)`. Named release costs are 0.20/0.21 for slightly early/late and 0.34/0.36 for very early/late. The quadratic fatigue term makes the accumulated match fatigue visible in make probability and makes recovery increase it again. The existing `MatchSession` release path (intentionally read-only for this task) already incorporates fatigue into charge and effective contest; the compatibility adapter does not apply that same fatigue twice. A second explicit physics overload accepts raw fatigue for callers that have not pre-adjusted it.

## 2. What the final five seconds of a real shot clock change

A shot clock changes the decision policy, not just the scoreboard. Skinner's NBA shot-selection study formulates the choice as an optimal-stopping problem: with the final possible opportunity, a team should take even a low-quality shot; with more opportunities left, it can wait for a better look. The study compares that threshold model to NBA play-by-play and finds teams shoot less often early in the clock than the model predicts. [Brian Skinner, "The Problem of Shot Selection in Basketball," PLOS ONE (2012)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0030776)

Csapo and Raab analyzed 94,056 NBA attempts from 666 games (2011–12 through 2013–14), using shot-clock time and defensive-pressure measures. Their reported field-goal rates span 25.29%–37.74% for shots with 1–5 seconds left, versus 48.04%–60.81% with 20–24 seconds left. The clock's last seconds coincide with harder, more pressured attempts; shot-clock time is not merely a score-state flag. [Csapo & Raab, "Hand down, Man down," PLOS ONE (2014)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0114184)

A more recent ShotTracker analysis of 2025–26 NCAA Division I data makes the behavioral changes explicit: with five or fewer seconds, men's guard share of attempts rises by four percentage points and men's three-point-attempt rate rises from 39.6% to 43.5%; men's bigs' field-goal percentage falls from 49.9% to 38.5%. This is college data, not NBA data, so it is a directional cross-check rather than an NBA calibration target. [ShotTracker, "What our Data Says About Clutch Shooting in Division I Basketball: Part 2" (2026)](https://shottracker.com/clutch-shooting-in-division-i-basketball-part-2/)

**Design takeaway:** late-clock behavior is about shot selection and who creates the attempt as well as about conversion. The requested shot model does not take the clock as an input, and this work does not change match flow; the clock evidence is kept as context rather than smuggled into player skill or collision physics.

## 3. Fast projectiles, substeps and thin Minecraft geometry

The most directly relevant Minecraft precedent found is Nukkit-MOT pull request #1298. It documents a concrete failure mode in a slender projectile: ten substeps that checked only endpoint AABBs could still skip a 0.125-block pane when a projectile moved about 0.315 blocks per substep. The fix sweeps the projectile through each substep against the actual multipart collision shapes, expanding each shape by the projectile's half-extents (the Minkowski-sum form of AABB-versus-AABB collision). It also broadens the candidate scan to the swept AABB. [Nukkit-MOT, "fix(entity): sweep slender projectiles through thin panes" (#1298)](https://github.com/MemoriesOfTime/Nukkit-MOT/pull/1298)

**Implementation and test:** substeps alone are not a proof against tunnelling. Live ball motion now selects enough intervals to keep each displacement at or below 0.20 blocks (subject to a work cap), and `BasketballEntity` ray-traces every full interval through Bukkit's block collision shapes. The pure regression test runs the matching 40 m/s case—2 blocks in one 20 Hz tick—against a one-block wall with a swept, radius-expanded AABB. It verifies ten intervals and first contact at one ball radius before the wall face. The project helper is conservative at corners; Bukkit's shape-aware ray trace remains the live collision authority. The cited Nukkit fix is a Minecraft precedent, not a Paper/Bukkit implementation.

## 4. Basketball rebound response: normal restitution and tangential damping

Restitution is a property of a pair of colliding surfaces and is a speed ratio along the impact normal; it is not one universal "bounciness" constant for the ball. FIBA's equipment test gives the ball a 1.2–1.4 m rebound from an approximately 1.8 m drop on the playing floor, which corresponds to a speed restitution of roughly 0.82–0.88. Direct tests on maple flooring report 0.82–0.83 over dribble-like impacts. [FIBA Official Basketball Rules 2020, Basketball Equipment](https://files.ffbb.com/sites/default/files/otm_regelement_jeu/fibaofficialbasketballrules2020_basketballequipment_v1.0_yellowtracking.pdf); [Peacock & Pearson, "Basketball Surfaces and Coefficient of Restitution" (2015)](https://www.isjos.org/pdfs/ISJOS_v9_p5.pdf)

For backboard impacts, Okubo and Hubbard report measured normal impact/rebound speed pairs of 3.02/2.40, 2.30/1.92 and 1.92/1.73 m/s for different spin conditions. Those ratios are approximately 0.80, 0.83 and 0.90; 0.84 is a defensible representative value, not a universal constant. Their work also estimates friction coefficients around 0.5 for a leather ball on the rim and 0.6 on tempered glass. [Okubo & Hubbard, "Identification of basketball parameters for a simulation model," Procedia Engineering (2010)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1877705810003991)

The rim's support system is tested differently: FIBA specifies 35%–50% impact-energy absorption. Interpreting the complementary 50%–65% as rebound energy gives an effective speed ratio of sqrt(0.50–0.65), about 0.71–0.81. This is a system-level proxy, not a measured ball/rim coefficient for every contact point. A representative gameplay value of 0.76 sits near the middle of that range. [FIBA Official Basketball Rules 2020, Basketball Equipment](https://files.ffbb.com/sites/default/files/otm_regelement_jeu/fibaofficialbasketballrules2020_basketballequipment_v1.0_yellowtracking.pdf)

A normal-only response flips or scales the inward normal component and leaves tangential velocity unchanged. On the rim that produces a ball that skates around the edge with no energy loss along the contact, which looks like a sticky/rail-bound ball rather than a real bounce. Okubo and Hubbard's model explicitly distinguishes slipping from non-slipping and spinning from non-spinning contact, and their measurements include friction; a plausible game approximation therefore needs tangential damping as well as a normal coefficient. [Okubo & Hubbard (2010)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1877705810003991); [Okubo & Hubbard, "Rebounds of basketball field shots," Sports Engineering (2015)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12283-014-0165-z)

**Chosen first-class constants and rationale (now used by the physics code and shipped config defaults):**

- **Rim restitution: 0.76.** Mid-range effective speed response derived from the FIBA ring-support energy-absorption requirement; a contact proxy, not a claim of a universal rim coefficient.
- **Backboard restitution: 0.84.** Rounded representative of the measured 0.80–0.90 normal-speed ratios under three spin conditions.
- **Floor restitution: 0.83.** Within both the measured maple-floor range (0.82–0.83) and the regulation range (0.82–0.88).
- **Tangential friction:** rim 0.50, backboard 0.60, floor 0.55. The first two follow the measured ball/rim and ball/glass estimates; floor friction is a gameplay value to stop persistent skating. Contacts preserve the separating normal component and apply a bounded slip impulse instead of scaling the whole velocity vector.
- **Rim escape:** a penetrated near-zero-normal-speed contact is depenetrated and given a deterministic 0.025-block/tick outward nudge. This is a numerical safeguard against a ball persisting on one edge, not a claim that real balls can never balance or lodge between real pieces of hoop hardware.

Tests cover named restitution values, tangential energy loss, exact-centreline escape, and the single-block tunnelling case. The pack/audio/resource SHA-1 sync and JUnit build were run after implementation.
